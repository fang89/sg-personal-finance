A Singapore household's money guide

Set it up once, then stop optimising.

Most personal finance content in Singapore sells you complexity. For an ordinary salaried household, nearly all of the value sits in a handful of decisions you make once: fund the CPF floor, own the world through one Irish-domiciled fund, stop leaking money at the card terminal. When the family grows, hire help properly, claim every dollar set aside for a new baby, insure the income everyone depends on, and file for every relief you are owed.

1.1 — CPF A guaranteed 4% floor and a tax deduction you can take every year.

Fund MediSave and Special Account until the Basic Retirement Sum is in sight

Before any market exposure, build the part of your retirement that cannot fall. CPF's Special, MediSave and Retirement Accounts pay a floor of 4% per annum, government-backed, and the cash you put in there is deductible against income tax in the same year.

In short
  • Top up MediSave toward the $79,000 Basic Healthcare Sum and your Special Account toward the Basic Retirement Sum.
  • Up to $8,000 a year of cash top-ups is tax-deductible — worth $560 to $1,760 depending on your bracket.
  • Top-ups are irreversible and locked to 65. Reach BRS first, then decide whether to go further.

The 2026 numbers

Your retirement sums are locked to the year you turn 55 — the figure that matters to you is the one in force in your cohort year, not today's. Today's:

CPF retirement sums — members turning 55 in 2026
SumAmountWhat it buys you
BRS — Basic $110,200 About $950/month for life from 65 on CPF LIFE Standard. Requires a property with lease to 95 to withdraw above it.
FRS — Full $220,400 Twice BRS. This is also the ceiling on how much you may top up your Special Account with cash before 55.

BHS — Basic Healthcare $79,000 The MediSave ceiling for 2026, up 4.6% from $75,500. Fixed for life at the figure in force when you turn 65.
Why BRS and not FRS

BRS is the point where CPF LIFE covers a genuinely basic retirement without you having to think about it again. Everything above BRS is a choice between a risk-free 4% locked to 65, and money you control. Reaching BRS first, then deciding, is a cleaner sequence than committing to FRS at 30 and regretting the liquidity at 45. Top-ups are irreversible.

The two levers, and what each one is capped at

There are exactly two cash top-ups worth knowing. Both are made from your own bank account, both earn the 4% floor, and both draw on the same $8,000 relief.

LeverGoes toYour cap in 2026Notes
RSTU top-up Special Account $220,400 − your SA Counts directly toward the Retirement Account created at 55. This is the lever that gets you to BRS.
VC-MA top-up MediSave Account $79,000 − your MA Also within the CPF Annual Limit of $37,740 across all contributions. Pays for hospital bills and MediShield/Integrated Shield premiums.

Filling MediSave has a second-order effect worth understanding: once MA hits the BHS, your mandatory monthly contributions that would have gone to MA overflow into your Special Account instead (Ordinary Account, once you are 55+). Below 55, capping MediSave quietly redirects part of your payroll into the account that counts toward BRS.

What the tax relief is actually worth

Up to $8,000 of relief for cash top-ups to your own SA/RA/MA, plus up to another $8,000 for top-ups to parents, parents-in-law, grandparents, spouse or siblings. It is a deduction, not a rebate, so its value is your marginal rate:

Read that last column carefully. A taxpayer in the 15% band who tops up $8,000 has made $1,200 back immediately, and then the $8,000 compounds at 4% for decades. Nothing else in this guide pays that in year one. If your $8,000 straddles a bracket boundary the saving is blended across the two rates.

Three things that will trip you up

The $8,000 is shared, not per-account. $5,000 to SA and $5,000 to MA gets you $8,000 of relief, not $10,000.

All reliefs together are capped at $80,000 per YA. If you are already near the cap through CPF contributions, NSman, spouse and child reliefs, a top-up buys you nothing in tax.

Matched top-ups do not double-dip. Top-ups attracting the Matched Retirement Savings Scheme grant lost relief eligibility from YA2026; Matched MediSave Scheme top-ups made from 1 Jan 2026 lose it from YA2027. The grant is worth more than the relief — just do not plan on both.

Interest, and the part everyone forgets

  • OA 2.5%, SA/MA/RA 4% — both floors, and the 4% floor is legislated through 31 December 2026.
  • Extra 1% on your first $60,000 of combined balances (max $20,000 from OA) if you are under 55. So the first $60,000 is really earning 5% in SA.
  • At 55, extra 2% on the first $30,000 and 1% on the next $30,000 — up to 6% on the first tranche.
  • The Special Account is gone at 55. Since 19 January 2025, SA closes at 55: balances move to the Retirement Account up to your cohort FRS, and any remainder drops to OA at 2.5%. Money you top up above FRS does not stay at 4%.

1.2 — ETF investing IBKR plus one or two Irish-domiciled accumulating funds. Set up once.
The two reasons domicile matters The funds Setting up IBKR Singapore stocks and the STI

Buy the world through Ireland, not through the United States

Above your CPF floor, the job is broad equity exposure at the lowest total cost. For a Singapore resident, the domicile of the fund is worth more than the choice of index — and picking the wrong one costs you money every year you hold it, plus a US tax filing your estate would rather not deal with.

In short
  • Buy IWDA (+ EIMI at ~10%), or just VWRA for one line — all Irish-domiciled, accumulating.
  • Irish domicile halves US dividend withholding (15% vs 30%) and keeps you out of US estate tax.
  • Use IBKR Singapore on Tiered pricing, convert SGD→USD on IDEALPRO, buy the LSE USD line monthly.

The two reasons domicile matters

US dividend withholding 15% Through an Irish UCITS fund, under the US–Ireland treaty. A US-domiciled ETF pays 30% — Singapore has no US tax treaty.
Annual drag saved ≈0.25% 15 points of withholding on a ~1.7% world dividend yield. Larger than the fund's whole management fee.
US estate tax exposure $0 Irish funds are not US-situs assets. Hold US-domiciled ETFs and anything above US$60,000 at death can face rates to 40%, with a Form 706-NA filing.

The estate point is the one people dismiss and should not. There is no US–Singapore estate tax treaty. A Singapore resident holding VOO or VTI has built a US-situs estate; their executor must file with the IRS before the shares can be released. IWDA and EIMI simply are not in that net.

Why accumulating, not distributing

Singapore does not tax capital gains or foreign dividends in an individual's hands, so a distributing fund costs you nothing in tax here — it costs you friction. An accumulating share class reinvests inside the fund at institutional cost: no dividend landing in USD, no FX conversion, no odd-lot re-purchase, no cash sitting idle, nothing to remember. That is the entire argument, and it is enough.

Listing currency is not currency risk

IWDA trades on the LSE in USD as IWDA and in GBP as SWDA, and on Euronext Amsterdam in EUR. Same fund, same assets, same returns. The currency of the line you buy affects your FX conversion cost and nothing else. Your actual currency exposure comes from the underlying companies, and it is unavoidable in a global fund. Do not buy a GBP-hedged share class thinking it protects a Singaporean.

Setting up IBKR

  1. Open with Interactive Brokers Singapore Pte Ltd, the MAS-licensed entity. Apply online; you will need your NRIC/FIN, proof of address, and answers on income and trading experience. There is no minimum balance and no inactivity fee for IBKR Pro accounts.
  2. Choose Tiered pricing, not Fixed. For LSE-listed ETFs, Tiered commissions start around USD 0.35 per trade against a USD 1.70 minimum on Fixed. On a $1,000 monthly purchase that difference is most of your transaction cost.
  3. Fund in SGD by FAST or PayNow to your IBKR deposit details. Bank transfer, not card.
  4. Convert SGD to USD inside IBKR on the IDEALPRO venue — commission around 0.2 basis points with a USD 2.00 minimum, at near-interbank rates. Converting at your bank first, or letting the broker auto-convert on settlement, costs many multiples of this.
  5. Buy the LSE USD line with a limit order. London runs 08:00–16:30 local: 15:00–23:30 Singapore time in summer, 16:00–00:30 in winter. Avoid the first and last fifteen minutes, when spreads are widest. Irish-domiciled ETFs are exempt from UK stamp duty, so there is no 0.5% charge.
  6. Automate the boredom. Same day each month, same order, no reading the news first. Then close the app.

The five expensive mistakes

Buying VOO, VTI or SPY as a Singapore resident. Buying the distributing class by accident — check for "(Acc)" in the full fund name. Converting SGD at your bank's retail rate. Choosing Fixed pricing and paying five times the commission. Adding a third, fourth and fifth fund until you own an expensive index of your own design.

Singapore stocks and the STI

The Straits Times Index (STI) tracks Singapore's 30 largest listed companies. It is a narrow index. DBS, OCBC and UOB together make up about half of its members' combined market value, and most of the rest is Singtel, property trusts and a few conglomerates. MSCI World already holds Singapore at a fraction of one percent of the index, which is about right for a market this size.

You do not need more than that. Your salary, CPF and home are already Singapore assets. If you still want a home tilt for the dividends, which Singapore companies pay tax-free, buy the whole index through one of the two STI ETFs on SGX, SPDR STI ETF (ES3) or Nikko AM Singapore STI ETF (G3B), rather than picking banks. Keep it a small slice next to IWDA, not a replacement for it.

1.3 — Credit card One flat card. No categories, no minimum spend, no monthly arithmetic.
The shortlist The rules that matter more than the rate

One uncapped cashback card, and never think about it again

This is the smallest of the three levers, and it is the one people spend the most time on. The whole point of a no-frills card is that it removes a recurring decision. A flat rate on everything, no minimum spend, no cap, no category table taped inside your wallet.

In short
  • One uncapped flat-rate cashback card (1.5–1.7%) for every payment you can put on a card.
  • Ask for the annual-fee waiver every year — the waiver matters more than the rate.
  • Pay the full balance by GIRO. One month of 27% interest wipes out years of cashback.

The rules that matter more than the rate

  • Ask for the fee waiver every year. One phone call or an app request. If a bank refuses, that is your signal to move the spend to the fee-waived card and cancel.
  • Never revolve a balance. Card interest in Singapore runs around 26–28% per annum and a late payment fee is roughly $100. One missed month erases several years of 1.7%.
  • Pay by GIRO on the full statement balance. Not the minimum. Set it once at application.
  • Put everything through the one card — insurance premiums, town council, phone, groceries — which is exactly why the near-zero-exclusion cards are worth their acceptance quirks.
  • Ignore the sign-up bonus in your comparison. It is one-off; the rate is forever.
Deliberately out of scope

Miles cards, minimum-spend cashback cards (UOB One, OCBC 365, HSBC Live+) and multi-card category stacking all pay more than 1.7% — to people who will track spend by category every month and hit the thresholds. That is a real strategy and a real hobby. This page assumes you do not want the hobby, and the cost of that choice is a few hundred dollars a year.

Insure the income your family depends on, and little else

Insurance is where Singapore households lose the most money quietly, mostly by buying savings plans dressed up as protection. Treat it as protection only. The government already covers basic hospital bills and severe disability for everyone. On top of that you need a hospital plan for the ward you would actually choose, and enough term life cover to replace your income until the children are grown.

In short
  • Keep MediShield Life and CareShield Life. Both are automatic. Add an Integrated Shield Plan for each family member, paid mostly from MediSave.
  • For each working parent, buy term life cover of about 9× annual income and critical illness cover of about 4× (MAS guide), running until the youngest child is independent.
  • Keep protection under 15% of income. Do not use insurance to save or invest. That is what CPF and ETFs are for.

What you already have

Every Singapore Citizen and PR already holds some cover through CPF. Check it before buying anything, because it changes how much private cover you need.

Many employers also give group term life and hospital cover. Count it while you have it, but do not build your plan on it: it ends when you change job, and that is often when you are least insurable.

Hospital cover: Shield plans and riders

An Integrated Shield Plan (IP) is a private insurer's plan built on top of MediShield Life, covering A or B1 wards in public hospitals or a private hospital. The MediShield Life part of the premium is paid entirely from MediSave. MediSave also pays the private part up to a yearly limit:

Age 40 or below $300 a year of MediSave toward the private part of the premium
Age 41 to 70 $600 a year
Age 71 and above $900 a year. Anything above the limit is paid in cash.

A plan for a public-hospital A ward is the sensible default for most families. The premium for a young adult is usually covered by MediSave, so it costs nothing in cash. A private-hospital plan costs much more, and the gap widens sharply after 60. Choose the ward you would really use, and make sure you can still afford the premium at 70.

Riders changed on 1 April 2026

A rider pays the deductible and co-insurance that the IP leaves to you, and it is always paid in cash. Riders sold from 1 April 2026 can no longer cover the deductible ($1,500–$3,500 a year depending on the ward). You still pay at least 5% of the bill, but that share is capped at at least $6,000 a year (previously $3,000) for panel or pre-authorised treatment. In return, new riders are expected to cost about 30% less: around $600 a year less for private-hospital riders and $200 less for public-hospital riders. Riders bought before 27 November 2025 are unchanged. Riders bought between then and 31 March 2026 must switch to the new rules by the first renewal after 1 April 2028.

A rider is optional. If you hold a few thousand dollars in cash or in MediSave for the deductible and co-payment, the IP alone covers the large, ruinous bills, which is what insurance is for.

Life and critical illness cover

MAS's Basic Financial Planning Guide gives three rules of thumb, based on LIA's protection gap study:

Death and total permanent disability 9× annual income in cover
Critical illness 4× annual income in cover
Total protection premiums ≤ 15% of income. MAS assumes term plans here; whole life plans cost more.

Subtract what you already hold, such as DPS, employer group cover, and invested savings you would happily spend. Then buy the rest as term cover that runs until your youngest child is about 25, or until the mortgage is paid off, whichever is later. Cover is needed most when the children are young, so a single policy to age 65 or 70 is usually enough.

"Buy term and invest the rest" is a common approach, not official advice. MoneySense simply notes that term is usually the most affordable protection and that bundled products generally cost more. The cheapest route is Direct Purchase Insurance (DPI). These are commission-free term policies of $50,000 to $400,000 per insurer, compared side by side on compareFIRST. For larger amounts, compare quotes from a few insurers or a fee-based adviser.

Things that cost people money

Buying an ILP or endowment as a "savings plan", then surrendering it in year three. Cancelling an old policy before the new one is approved. Leaving out a medical condition on the form, which lets the insurer refuse a claim later. Forgetting that every life policy has a 14-day free-look period from when you receive the policy document, in which you can cancel for a near-full refund.

Insuring the baby

  • MediShield Life covers your baby from birth, including congenital and neonatal conditions. The $5,000 MediSave Grant for Newborns pays its premiums to about age 21.
  • Add an Integrated Shield Plan early. Insurers usually accept newborns from 15 days old, or after discharge from hospital if later. A condition found before you apply can be excluded for life, so apply in the first weeks. The premium for a child's public-hospital plan is usually within the MediSave limit.
  • Maternity policies can usually be bought from about 13 weeks of pregnancy. They pay fixed sums for listed pregnancy complications and congenital conditions in the baby. They are optional, and most useful if you want cover before birth.
  • Life insurance on a child is rarely needed. Nobody depends on the child's income. Spend the premium on the parents' term cover instead.

1.5 — Tax Every relief a family can claim, the two voluntary ones worth funding before 31 December, and who should claim what.
The rates Every relief a family can claim SRS: tax-deferred, and often tax-free Which spouse claims what The tax year, in order

Claim every relief, then fund the two that pay you back

Singapore's income tax is low and simple, and for a salaried household most of it is filled in automatically. What is left to you is the reliefs. Some you only have to claim. Two you can choose to fund, CPF top-ups and SRS, and they return your marginal tax rate straight away. Once children arrive, it also matters which parent claims what.

In short
  • A relief is worth your marginal rate; a rebate is worth its face value. All reliefs together are capped at $80,000 a year.
  • Before 31 December, consider a CPF cash top-up (up to $8,000, plus $8,000 for family) and an SRS contribution (up to $15,300). At an 11.5% rate the two together save over $2,600.
  • Put shareable child reliefs and the Parenthood Tax Rebate on the parent with the higher marginal rate. File by 18 April and pay by GIRO in 12 interest-free instalments.

Reliefs that are gone

Foreign Domestic Worker Levy Relief ended from YA2025, so the helper levy no longer reduces tax. Course Fees Relief ended from YA2026. Life Insurance Relief still exists, but only for people whose compulsory CPF is under $5,000 a year, which excludes most employees.

SRS: tax-deferred, and often tax-free

The Supplementary Retirement Scheme is an account you open at DBS, OCBC or UOB. Every dollar paid in is deducted from that year's income. The catch is that it is locked until the statutory retirement age in force when you make your first contribution, which is 64 for accounts opened from 1 July 2026.

  • Only half of each withdrawal is taxed, and you can spread withdrawals over 10 years. Taking out $40,000 a year makes $20,000 taxable, which is inside the 0% band if you have no other income. Done that way, the money is never taxed at all.
  • Early withdrawal is expensive. The whole amount is taxed, plus a 5% penalty, except on death, serious illness or bankruptcy.
  • Invest it. Left as cash, SRS earns next to nothing. The SRS bank's brokerage can buy SGX-listed ETFs such as the STI ETFs, plus unit trusts and Singapore Savings Bonds.
  • Worth it from about the 11.5% band. Below that, the CPF top-up (which also earns 4%) is the better first $8,000. Fund CPF first, then SRS.

Which spouse claims what

  • Parenthood Tax Rebate: put it on the higher-earning parent first. It offsets tax dollar for dollar and carries forward, so none is wasted, but the higher earner uses it up sooner.
  • Qualifying Child Relief: claim it on the parent with the higher marginal rate. Splitting only helps if it would otherwise push someone below their band.
  • Working Mother's Child Relief and Grandparent Caregiver Relief: the mother only. Check her reliefs do not exceed her income, or the excess is wasted.
  • Parent Relief: each parent can be claimed by only one child. Agree with your siblings who claims, and give it to whoever has the highest rate.
  • CPF top-ups: topping up a lower-earning spouse's CPF counts toward your family $8,000 relief, if they earned $8,000 or less the year before. That builds the lower earner's retirement and cuts the higher earner's tax.

The tax year, in order

  1. January to 31 December: make CPF top-ups, SRS contributions and donations. They count for the next year of assessment, so money paid in 2026 reduces the YA2027 bill. Paying early in the year also gives a CPF top-up more months of interest.
  2. From March: if you are on the No-Filing Service, IRAS pre-fills your return; if you are on Direct Notice of Assessment, the bill itself arrives from mid-March. Either way, log in and check the reliefs, especially after a birth.
  3. 1 March to 18 April: file through myTax Portal with Singpass. Add new children, the Parenthood Tax Rebate split and Parent Relief here.
  4. End April to September: the Notice of Assessment arrives. Object within 30 days if something is wrong.
  5. From May: on GIRO, the bill is paid in up to 12 interest-free monthly instalments on the 6th of each month.

3 — Hiring a helper A live-in domestic helper: what it really costs, what MOM requires, and the levy discount most families qualify for.

Hire a helper with your eyes open on the full monthly cost

A live-in migrant domestic worker (MDW) is the most common childcare and eldercare arrangement in Singapore. The salary is the smallest surprise. Most of the real cost is in the levy, upkeep, insurance and agency fee. The one large saving is a levy concession that applies automatically once a Singapore Citizen child under 16 lives with you, as long as you register the child.

In short
  • Budget about $1,000–$1,250 a month all-in, and $13,600–$18,400 in the first year including the agency fee.
  • The levy is $300 a month, or $60 if a citizen child under 16 (or a citizen aged 67+) lives with you. That is $2,880 a year saved, so register them in the FDW eService.
  • The FDW levy tax relief ended from YA2025. The concessionary levy is now the only support.

Who qualifies for the $60 levy

The eligible person must live with you and be registered as a household member in MOM's FDW eService. Any one of these qualifies:

  • A Singapore Citizen child under 16. A newborn counts from the day you add them.
  • A Singapore Citizen aged 67 or above, or a PR aged 67 or above if you or your spouse is a citizen.
  • A person with disabilities certified as needing help with at least one activity of daily living, with an AIC recommendation letter.

Each eligible person covers one helper, up to two concession helpers per household. The lower rate appears within two levy bills and is backdated to the day you updated the household. MOM does not apply it automatically, so do the update the week the baby's birth is registered.

Step by step

  1. Decide between an agency and a direct hire. A transfer helper (already working in Singapore) can be hired directly through the FDW eService with Singpass. That skips the Settling-In Programme and most of the agency fee.
  2. Attend the Employers' Orientation Programme if this is your first helper. Do it at least two working days before you apply. New or expectant mothers may send a representative.
  3. Apply in the FDW eService ($35) with her passport, your household details, income documents and her written consent. It takes about a week.
  4. Before she flies, send her the In-Principle Approval letter. Buy the $5,000 security bond (effective on her arrival date), the medical and personal accident insurance, and set up levy GIRO.
  5. Within 7 days of arrival she attends the Settling-In Programme. She may not start work until she has. Within 14 days she has a medical exam.
  6. Request issuance of the permit ($35) within two weeks. Register her fingerprints and photo if asked, and the card arrives within five working days.
  7. Register your under-16 child or 67+ parent in the eService for the $60 levy.

Your obligations as employer

  • A weekly rest day. If she agrees to work on it, pay at least one extra day's salary. Since 2023, at least one rest day a month cannot be bought out.
  • Pay salary within 7 days of the end of each month, by bank transfer into her own account. If you pay in cash, both of you sign a monthly record. Never hold her passport, bank book or money.
  • All medical costs are yours, including the six-monthly medical exam (6ME) at a clinic. Insurance premiums cannot be deducted from her pay.
  • Adequate food and housing: three meals a day, her own mattress and bedding, ventilation, privacy, and no cameras in her private space.
  • Expect well-being checks. A first-time helper has two interviews at the Centre for Domestic Employees in her first year, and MOM makes random doorstep visits with two weeks' notice.
Things that cost people their bond or permit

Missing two consecutive months of levy, which revokes the permit. Having her work at another address, such as your parents' flat. Lending her out part-time. Paying her late or in cash without records. Not sending her home when the permit ends.

If you do not want a live-in

Household Services Scheme companies send licensed workers part-time for cleaning, groceries and elder-minding. Child-minding was dropped from the pilot in March 2026. For an elderly parent who needs help with three or more daily activities, AIC's Home Caregiving Grant pays $200–$600 a month, and that grant can go toward a helper.

4 — Baby bonus Every cash grant, account top-up, leave week and tax rebate for a Singapore Citizen baby, and the few forms that unlock them.
The money, at a glance The CDA is the best return in this guide Parental leave Tax reliefs and rebates Childcare, health and housing What to do, and when

Claim everything the government sets aside for a new baby

A Singapore Citizen baby comes with tens of thousands of dollars of support: cash, a co-matched savings account, MediSave, 30 weeks of government-paid parental leave, and tax rebates. Very little of it is paid automatically. Most of it depends on one LifeSG enrolment, one bank account and one PayNow link, and on you actually putting money into the CDA.

In short
  • Enrol in the Baby Bonus scheme on LifeSG when you register the birth. That opens the CDA and starts the cash gift.
  • Deposit enough into the CDA to collect the full government match ($4,000 for a first child). It is a 100% instant return, and you can spend it on childcare and medical bills.
  • Take all 30 weeks of paid leave between you, and claim the Parenthood Tax Rebate ($5,000 to $20,000) in the next tax return.
The scheme is changing: born 1 Jan 2026 to 31 Mar 2027

At the National Day Rally on 23 August 2026 the government announced the SG Child Support Package. It replaces the Baby Bonus and Large Families schemes for every citizen child born from 1 April 2027, and is worth up to $62,000 per child regardless of birth order. It includes a $10,000 Baby Gift, Child Credits of $2,000 a year from age 1 to 16, CDA grants and matching, and a $10,000 PSEA top-up at 17.

Babies born 1 January 2026 to 31 March 2027 start on today's rules and move across automatically. The cash gift is topped up to $10,000 by 30 April 2027, Child Credits begin from April 2027, and the CDA match cap becomes $5,000 from 1 October 2027. The one thing you must do: set up PayNow on the child's birth certificate or NRIC number before 1 April 2027, because that is where the new payouts go.

The CDA is the best return in this guide

Every dollar you put into the Child Development Account, up to the cap, is matched instantly. A first child's parents who deposit $4,000 have $13,000 in the account once the $5,000 First Step Grant is added. The money is then spent on the things you were going to pay for anyway: infant care and childcare fees, kindergarten, medical bills at approved clinics and hospitals, MediShield Life premiums and early intervention. That makes it a 100% return in weeks, not years.

  • Open it at DBS/POSB, OCBC or UOB. You choose the bank in the LifeSG Baby Bonus application.
  • Deposit up to the match cap. Only your own deposits into the CDA are matched. The cash gift itself does not count toward the match.
  • Link the CDA to the infant care centre so fees come straight out of the account.
  • Matched deposits are accepted until the end of the year the child turns 12. Anything unspent moves to the child's Post-Secondary Education Account.

Childcare, health and housing

  • Infant care subsidy: $600 a month basic subsidy if the mother works, plus up to $710 more on a means test for household income up to $12,000 a month ($15,000 from 2027). Childcare from 18 months: $300 basic, plus up to $467 more. The subsidy is paid directly to the centre.
  • Health: the baby has MediShield Life from birth for life, congenital conditions included. Childhood vaccinations and developmental screenings are free at polyclinics and CHAS GP clinics.
  • Passport: the first one is free if you apply on MyICA before the baby's first birthday.
  • Housing: first-timer families with a child (or expecting one) get priority and an extra ballot for BTO flats. From the Feb 2027 launch, it is one extra ballot per citizen child. NDR 2026 also raised the income ceiling for housing grants to $16,000.
  • Helper: a citizen child under 16 at home cuts the levy on a domestic helper from $300 to $60 a month. See Hiring a helper.

What to do, and when

  1. During pregnancy: agree the shared parental leave split with your partner. Apply for a BTO flat under the parenthood priority if you are first-timers.
  2. Within 42 days of birth: register the birth on LifeSG and enrol in the Baby Bonus scheme in the same flow, choosing your CDA bank.
  3. Within 4 weeks of birth: reallocate the shared parental leave on LifeSG if you are not splitting it 5 + 5.
  4. Within a few weeks: open the CDA at the bank and make the first deposit. Add the baby to your MOM household for the helper levy concession, and apply for the free passport.
  5. Before 1 April 2027: set up PayNow on the baby's birth certificate number for the new Child Credits.
  6. Next tax season: claim the Parenthood Tax Rebate, Qualifying Child Relief and Working Mother's Child Relief in your tax return.
